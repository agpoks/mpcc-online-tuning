\begin{abstract}
Treating a model-predictive controller as a differentiable function of its cost
weights underpins a growing family of methods that tune those weights online.
The gradient they use rests on the envelope theorem, which is exact \emph{at a
solution}. Deployed controllers are not at a solution: they run a fixed, small
number of SQP steps per tick and warm-start from the previous one, which makes
them formally recurrent --- the warm start is hidden state --- so their
sensitivity to the cost weights contains a term the envelope theorem cannot
see. We set out to measure that term and find that \textbf{it does not matter}.
For a genuine real-time iteration the memoryless gradient is exact in direction
(cosine $1.0000$) and $16\%$ off in magnitude, which for a method whose step
size is a tuned hyperparameter is not a distinction. The assumption the
literature makes without stating it is therefore justified, and this is the
measurement that justifies it. We also report the test that appears to refute
it: approximating real-time iteration by capping an interior-point solver at
one iteration yields a gradient orthogonal to the memoryless one, not because
of solver memory but because none of those solves converge --- the iterate
moves an order of magnitude further than a converged step. Separately, we show
that an online tuner needs a predictive safety filter to survive its own
learning ($0$ crashes in $26$ episodes against $8/8$ without, and nine times
the distance covered), and that the filter converts a catastrophic failure into
a slow one rather than removing it.
\end{abstract}

\section{Related work}

\emph{MPC as a function approximator.} Gros and Zanon observe that one
parametrised optimal-control problem supplies a policy, a state value and an
action value at once, so a reinforcement-learning layer needs no critic network
--- the MPC already is one. The tooling that followed (\texttt{mpcrl},
MPC4RL) and the applications built on it (AD-MPCC for autonomous racing,
COAT-MPC and related Bayesian approaches for lap-time tuning) all inherit the
same gradient: the derivative of the optimal value with respect to the
parameters, taken at a solution via the envelope theorem.

\emph{What they share, and do not state.} Every method in that group
differentiates \emph{through a solution}. The envelope theorem is exact at one;
away from one it is not a theorem at all. A deployed controller does not reach
one --- it takes a fixed, small number of SQP steps per tick and warm-starts
from the previous solve. We have not found a paper in this family that states
solver convergence as an assumption, and Section~\ref{sec:rti} shows it is one.

\emph{Real-time iteration.} Diehl, Bock and Schl\"oder's RTI scheme is the
reason the assumption is interesting rather than academic: it deliberately
stops after one QP and applies the result, with a contraction argument in place
of convergence. \texttt{acados} implements it, and its contraction rate is the
same quantity that governs how far back the influence recursion of
Section~\ref{sec:theory} must be carried.

\emph{Learning-time safety.} A predictive safety filter (Wabersich and
Zeilinger) certifies a proposed input by exhibiting a backup trajectory that
ends in a set the system can remain in; control-barrier-function methods (Ames
et al.) and ASIF (Gurriet and Ames) certify by evaluating a function instead.
The distinction matters for us only in cost and conservatism --- our comparison
in Section~\ref{sec:safety} finds the \emph{model} each is given dominates the
choice of criterion.

\emph{Where this paper sits.} It adds no method. It measures a term everyone
else assumes away, reports that the term is negligible, and reports the
experiment that appears to show the opposite.

The point of departure is that every method in the first group differentiates
\emph{through a solution}. None states the convergence of the deployed solver
as an assumption, and Section~\ref{sec:rti} shows it is one.

\section{MPCC and the two errors}

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figures/mpcc_geometry.png}
  \caption{Model predictive contouring control tracks a \emph{path} and carries
  its own progress $s$ as a decision variable. The reference point $p(s)$ is
  therefore chosen by the solver, not obtained by projection, and the vector to
  the car splits into a contouring error $e_c$ across the path and a lag error
  $e_l$ along it. Without a penalty on $e_l$ the cheapest way to collect the
  progress reward is to run $s$ forward and leave the car behind.}
  \label{fig:geometry}
\end{figure}

MPCC tracks a \emph{path} and carries its own progress $s$ as a decision
variable, so the reference point is chosen by the solver rather than obtained
by projection, and the vector to the car splits into a contouring error across
the path and a lag error along it (Fig.~\ref{fig:geometry}). The cost, with
$\theta = \log(q_c, q_l, q_v, r_\delta, r_a, r_{\dot s})$ the learnable
weights,
\begin{equation}
  J = \sum_k q_c e_c^2 + q_l e_l^2 - q_v \dot s\,\Delta t
      + r_\delta \delta^2 + r_a a^2 + r_{\dot s}(\dot s - v)^2 .
  \label{eq:cost}
\end{equation}

\section{Method}\label{sec:method}

Every number in this paper comes from the setup described here. We state it in
full because the central claim is a \emph{negative} one --- that a term is
negligible --- and a negative result is only as strong as the conditions under
which the measurement was made.

\subsection{Controller}
The MPCC solves \eqref{eq:cost} over a horizon of $N = 12$ shooting nodes with
a \SI{0.15}{\second} interval, on the kinematic bicycle
$\dot x = v\cos\psi$, $\dot y = v\sin\psi$,
$\dot\psi = (v/L)\tan\delta$, $\dot v = a - c_d v$, integrated with RK4.
The progress variable carries $\dot s = v_s$ as a fourth control. Parameters:
$L = \SI{0.33}{\meter}$, $c_d = 0.15$, $|\delta| \le \SI{0.40}{\radian}$,
$|a| \le \SI{4}{\meter\per\second\squared}$,
$v \in [0, \SI{4}{\meter\per\second}]$. Track limits enter as a bound on the
contouring error, $|e_c| \le w - r_{\text{car}}$ with $w = \SI{0.75}{\meter}$
and $r_{\text{car}} = \SI{0.12}{\meter}$; the centreline is a periodic B-spline
in $s$, so the solver differentiates the path rather than projecting onto it.

Two solvers are used for the measurements below and the distinction matters
throughout: IPOPT run to convergence, and an SQP real-time iteration --- one
linearisation, one full QP, warm-started from the previous tick, with a
Gauss--Newton Hessian and CasADi's \texttt{qrqp}.

\textbf{The RTI used for Section~\ref{sec:rti} is hand-rolled, not
\texttt{acados}.} It implements the same scheme as \textsc{sqp\_rti}, and the
confirmation against \texttt{acados} itself is the check this paper's central
measurement owes. Since that measurement was taken we have built an
\texttt{acados} backend for the same OCP --- it code-generates, solves, and runs
at \SI{1.63}{\milli\second} mean against \SI{1.9}{\milli\second} for the
hand-rolled scheme --- so the check is now possible. \textbf{We have not yet
re-run it}, and state that plainly rather than let the caveat read as a
limitation of the tooling.

Two details of that port are worth recording because they change the
formulation rather than the plumbing. The progress reward $-q_v \dot s \Delta t$
is \emph{linear}, so it cannot be a least-squares residual: encoding it as one
squares it into a quadratic \emph{penalty} on progress. We therefore use an
external cost, which in turn forces an exact Hessian. And the reference path
enters our problem as a spline in $s$ evaluated \emph{inside} the OCP, so that
the solver chooses its own reference point; passing the reference as a stage
parameter instead --- the usual arrangement --- decouples $s$ from the path
within a solve, and that coupling is the mechanism the envelope gradient runs
through.

\subsection{Learnable parameters and the gradient}
$\theta \in \mathbb{R}^6$ is the \emph{logarithm} of the six weights in
\eqref{eq:cost}, which keeps them positive under an unconstrained step and makes
a step multiplicative --- the right behaviour for a scale parameter spanning
orders of magnitude. $\theta$ is a runtime parameter of the OCP, not a
structural one, so changing it does not rebuild the solver.

With $\mathcal{L} = f + \lambda^\top g + \mu^\top h$ the Lagrangian, the
envelope theorem gives, at a solution $(w^\star, \lambda^\star, \mu^\star)$,
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\mathrm{d}J^\star}{\mathrm{d}\theta}
  = \frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\theta}\bigg|_{w^\star,\lambda^\star,\mu^\star},
  \label{eq:envelope}
\end{equation}
the primal and dual variables held fixed. The cost is one evaluation of an
expression built once at construction, on quantities the solve already returned
--- $0.079\%$ of the solve. \textbf{We verify \eqref{eq:envelope} rather than
assume it}: against central finite differences on the optimal value, over $54$
components at states the controller actually visits during a run (not at points
placed on the centreline), the cosine is $0.99999$ and the relative error below
$10^{-3}$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:gradcheck}).

Because the MPC minimises a cost while the learner maximises a return, both the
value and its gradient carry a negation, $V = -J^\star$. Applying one and not
the other drives every weight the wrong way while resembling an over-large step
size; a directional unit test guards it.

\subsection{Outer loop}
The MPC supplies the policy, the state value and the action value from a single
problem: $\pi_\theta(s)$ is the first control of the solution, $V_\theta(s) =
-J^\star$, and $Q_\theta(s,a)$ is the same NLP with $u_0$ pinned to $a$ --- not
a second problem, just equality bounds on two decision variables. When
$a = \pi_\theta(s)$ the two coincide and the second solve is skipped, so it runs
only on ticks where exploration perturbed the action.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{TD($\lambda$) on the cost weights, one update per control tick}
\label{alg:tdlambda}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\State $e \gets 0$
\For{each control tick}
  \State solve MPCC at $s_t$ \Comment{gives $\pi_\theta(s_t)$, $V_\theta(s_t)$}
  \State $a_t \gets \pi_\theta(s_t) + \varepsilon$ \Comment{$\varepsilon$: actuator noise}
  \State $Q, \nabla_\theta Q \gets$ solve with $u_0 = a_t$, then \eqref{eq:envelope}
  \State apply $a_t$; observe $r_t$, $s_{t+1}$
  \State $\delta \gets \mathrm{clip}\!\left(r_t + \gamma V_\theta(s_{t+1}) - Q, \pm\delta_{\max}\right)$
  \State $\hat g \gets \mathrm{clip}\!\left(-\nabla_\theta Q / \mathrm{RMS}, \pm c\right)$
  \State $e \gets \gamma\lambda e + \hat g$
  \State $\theta \gets \mathrm{clip}\!\left(\theta + \alpha\,\delta\,e,\ \pm 6\right)$
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

Algorithm~\ref{alg:tdlambda} lists the loop. Three details are load-bearing and
were each a failure before they were a line of code.

\emph{The gradient is normalised by a running RMS.} $J^\star$ is a sum of
weighted squared errors with no natural scale, so neither has
$\nabla_\theta J^\star$, and a fixed $\alpha$ means nothing across problems.
Normalising makes $\alpha$ a step in relative terms.

\emph{The TD error is clipped, not only the gradient.} Leaving the track pays
$-5$ against roughly $+0.07$ for an ordinary tick, so the terminal transition
produces a $\delta$ two orders of magnitude larger than any other, arriving
multiplied by a trace that has been accumulating for ten steps. Unclipped, one
crash moves every weight further than the preceding episode did.

\emph{Traces, because the consequence of a wrong weight appears several ticks
after the tick it was wrong on.} One number per parameter, six parameters.

\subsection{Plant, task and reward}
The plant is a kinematic bicycle at \SI{20}{\hertz} with one deliberate
addition the controller does not model: a grip limit capping the yaw rate at
$a_{\text{lat,max}}\,\mu/v$, with $a_{\text{lat,max}} =
\SI{6}{\meter\per\second\squared}$. \textbf{The mismatch is the point} --- a
tyre limit the controller has never heard of is exactly what should show up as
cost weights that are wrong for the real vehicle, and a shared model between
plant and controller would make the question unaskable.

The reward is the \emph{real} objective and deliberately not the MPCC's internal
cost: metres of centreline progress per tick, minus $5$ for leaving the track,
which ends the episode. If the two were the same quantity the MPC would already
be optimal for it by construction and there would be nothing to learn.

Episodes are $400$ steps (\SI{20}{\second}) on a \SI{26.7}{\meter} oval with
\SI{2.5}{\meter} corners. Weights are initialised deliberately badly ---
$q_l = 200$, $q_v = 0.05$ --- so the controller crawls and has somewhere to
improve from.

\subsection{Protocol, and what is randomised}
The only stochastic element in the loop is the actuator exploration noise, so a
``seed'' is a draw of that stream; the plant, track and initialisation are
deterministic. This makes seed-to-seed variation a clean measure of the
learner's reliability rather than of the task's.

\textbf{Where a result is single-seed we say so.} The safety-filter comparison
of Section~\ref{sec:safety} is one seed and should be read as an existence
result --- it shows the failure and its removal, not the size of the effect.
The situation-dependent weighting we report separately is over six seeds with
its spread, and we regard that as the standard the filter result has yet to
meet.

\subsection{Reproducibility}
The implementation is Python with CasADi and IPOPT; no simulator is required.
Every table names the script that produces it, and each writes a JSON record
under \texttt{benchmarks/results/}. Solver timings are reported as mean
\emph{and} worst case, because a controller whose mean fits its budget and whose
tail does not is a controller that misses deadlines.

\section{Warm starts are memory}\label{sec:theory}

Write one solver tick as
\begin{equation}
  w_{t+1} = \Phi(w_t, s_t, \theta), \qquad u_t = g(w_t),
  \label{eq:phi}
\end{equation}
with $w$ the primal--dual iterate, $\Phi$ one RTI/SQP step and $g$ the
projection onto the first control. Compare a recurrent network,
$h_{t+1} = F(h_t, x_t, \theta)$. \emph{They are the same object}: the solver is
a recurrent cell whose update happens to be a Newton step. The influence
therefore obeys the recursion real-time recurrent learning uses,
\begin{equation}
  J_{t+1} = \underbrace{\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial w}}_{D_t} J_t
            + \frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial\theta},
  \qquad J_t = \frac{\partial w_t}{\partial\theta},
  \label{eq:rtrl}
\end{equation}
and the sensitivity a policy-gradient update needs is
$\partial u_t/\partial\theta = (\partial g/\partial w)\,J_t$.

The envelope theorem is the $J_t \to$ fixed-point limit of
\eqref{eq:rtrl}: it is what \eqref{eq:phi} converges to when $\Phi$ is iterated
to convergence at every tick, so that $w_t$ carries no information about the
past. Because $D_t$ is the Jacobian of a contracting Newton map, $\|J_t\|$
loses history geometrically at rate $\rho(D)$, which is what makes a truncated
version of \eqref{eq:rtrl} affordable --- the same argument that justifies
truncation in RTRL.

\subsection{Isolating the solver}
The quantity of interest is how much of $\partial u_0/\partial\theta$ arrives
\emph{through the warm start}. Measuring it in closed loop is not possible:
perturbing $\theta$ changes the input, which drives the car to a different
state, and the resulting change in $u_0$ then mixes the solver's memory with
the plant's. The two effects are confounded by construction.

We therefore \textbf{replay a recorded state sequence}. A nominal run is logged;
each variant is then driven through the \emph{same} $s_0,\dots,s_T$ with the
plant out of the loop, so the only path from $\theta$ to the iterate at tick $T$
is the chain of warm starts. The comparison is:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{memoryless}: at each tick, solve to convergence from a
        \emph{cold} start. No state crosses the tick boundary; this is what the
        envelope theorem describes.
  \item \textbf{warm-started and converged}: carry the previous solution as the
        initial guess, then converge. State crosses the boundary but is
        annihilated by convergence --- a control that separates ``warm start''
        from ``not converged''.
  \item \textbf{SQP-RTI}: carry the iterate and take exactly one linearisation
        and one full QP. This is the deployed case.
\end{enumerate}
Sensitivities are obtained by central finite differences on $u_0$ with respect
to each component of $\theta$, through the whole replayed chain, so the measured
$J$ includes whatever the warm start contributes. We report $\lVert J\rVert$ and
the cosine to case~1, because for a gradient method with a tuned step size the
direction is what has to survive and the magnitude is absorbed by $\alpha$.

Separately we measure the decay directly: perturb the warm start alone, hold
$\theta$ fixed, and track $\lVert\Delta w\rVert$ forward. This gives $\rho$
empirically rather than from $\rho(D)$, and it is the evidence that the warm
start really is memory (Fig.~\ref{fig:rti}, right) --- which matters, because the
paper's claim is that this memory does not reach the gradient, not that it is
absent.

\subsection{Result}
Table~\ref{tab:rti}. With a genuine real-time iteration --- one full QP per
tick, warm-started, as in \texttt{acados}'s \textsc{sqp\_rti} --- the
sensitivity is \emph{identical in direction} to the memoryless one and $16\%$
smaller in magnitude. One QP step from a good warm start lands very close to
the converged solution: the mean step is $\lVert\Delta w\rVert = 0.748$ against
a converged step of $0.748$.

\subsection{The conclusion, stated as an assumption}
\begin{quote}\itshape
Differentiating a model-predictive controller as though it were converged is
justified for real-time iteration. This is an assumption the online-tuning
literature makes without stating; it holds, and the margin is $16\%$ in
magnitude and nothing in direction.
\end{quote}

\section{The test that says otherwise}\label{sec:trap}

There is an obvious way to approximate real-time iteration --- cap an
interior-point solver at one iteration --- and it gives the opposite answer.
Under that substitution the gradient appears \emph{orthogonal} to the
memoryless one, $\cos = 0.001$ on the kinematic bicycle and $-0.36$ on the
fitted-tyre plant, which reads as a decisive confirmation that solver memory
matters.

It is not. Those solves have failed:
\begin{itemize}
  \item $0$ of $41$ report success;
  \item the iterate moves by $8.19$ on average, an order of magnitude
        \emph{further} than a converged step ($0.748$), because an
        interior-point method's first step from a warm start is large and not
        yet meaningful.
\end{itemize}
The measured quantity is the parameter-sensitivity of a non-solution. Real-time
iteration is an SQP scheme --- linearise once, solve one full QP --- and the
distinction inverts the result. We report this because the wrong experiment is
cheaper and more natural than the right one, and because we ran it first.

\section{Surviving your own tuning}\label{sec:safety}

Unconstrained online tuning destroys the controller. From the paper's
initialisation the tuner improves the distance covered for five episodes
($73 \to 79$\,m) and then leaves the track on every subsequent episode, with
$q_c$ --- the weight holding the car near the path --- falling from $10$ to
$0.41$. Most of the damage occurs in episode~0, in which $q_v$ moves by a factor
of $222$.

A predictive safety filter removes the failure without touching the learner.

  \centering
  \caption{Online tuning with and without a predictive safety filter, same seed,
  26 episodes. \textbf{Single seed}: an existence result, not an effect size ---
  see Section~\ref{sec:method}.}
  \label{tab:safety}
  \begin{tabular}{lrr}
    \toprule
     & no filter & with filter \\
    \midrule
    last 8 episodes, mean distance & \SI{7.6}{\meter} & \SI{68.1}{\meter} \\

    steps overridden, episodes 0--3 & --- & $0\%$ \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}

Two honest qualifications. The filter is \emph{invisible} until it is needed:
episodes 0--3 are bit-identical to the unfiltered run. But it converts a crash
into a slow degradation rather than preventing the degradation --- $q_c$ still
falls to $0.14$ by episode~20 --- because the consequence of pushing it down is
absorbed by the filter and never reaches the return. Evaluating the resulting
weights \emph{without} the filter gives the same distance as with it, so the
tuner did not learn to depend on the filter; it simply drifted.

\section{Conclusion}

A deployed MPC is formally a recurrent system, and we expected its gradient with
respect to its own cost weights to differ from the one the envelope theorem
provides. It does not, to any degree that matters: for real-time iteration the
direction is exact and the magnitude is $16\%$ low. The contribution is
therefore a negative one --- an assumption the field relies on silently, checked
and found sound --- together with a warning about the substitution that makes it
look unsound, and a safety filter that makes online tuning survivable in the
first place.
